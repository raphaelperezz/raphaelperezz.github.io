01 / Senior design capstone · Thermal & mechanical

Cow Rumen Digestion Simulator

Digest-A-Bull is an instrumented, sealed benchtop bioreactor that recreates a cow's rumen so an agricultural lab can run repeatable anaerobic-fermentation studies. As mechanical design lead on a 10-person capstone I owned the heating, the mixing, and the sealed glass vessel and lid. PID-controlled immersion heaters held the culture to within 0.1 °C of setpoint from 30 to 50 °C, the impeller I sized kept corn-syrup-thick fluid turning at speed, and the enclosure passed an IPX-5 water-ingress test, the conditions a usable rumen simulation depends on.

±0.1 °CSteady-state temperature control via PID, across 30 to 50 °C.
PID thermal controlAxial-flow mixingIPX-5 sealingEnergy balanceInstrumentationSolidWorks
Show full breakdownHide breakdown

The problem

Rumen-fermentation studies only give valid data if the biology is held steady, and the client's needs were specific: hold temperature close to setpoint and reach it within an hour, keep thick, settling contents mixing at a set speed, and stay sealed and anaerobic while logging temperature, pressure, methane, and pH. The team selected each subsystem with Pugh-chart trade studies across competing concepts; I took the heating, the mixing, and the vessel.

Approach

I sized the heaters from a lumped energy balance: bringing the charge from 20 to 50 °C takes about 188.5 kJ, which I covered with two submerged rods totaling 150 W under PID control, while a thermal-resistance model of the lid, walls, and base set the expected heat-up time. For mixing I sized a pitched-blade, axial-flow impeller and its shaft from the Reynolds number and torque in roughly corn-syrup-viscosity fluid, driven by a 12 V motor under proportional speed control. The vessel is borosilicate glass with a sealed lid carrying a pressure-relief path and ports for a PT100 probe, differential pressure, methane (MQ-4), and pH.

Lumped vessel energy balance used to size the heater duty, about 188.5 kJ to bring the charge to 50 °C, before any hardware existed.

Key tradeoffs

  • Immersion rods over a jacket or unit heater: the legacy unit heater scorched the fluid boundary layer; two submerged rods add heat gently below the fluid line and keep working if one fails.
  • Pitched-blade axial impeller over radial: axial flow moves thick contents top to bottom to keep solids suspended, rather than just spinning the surface.
  • Borosilicate glass with 316L wetted internals: scientists needed to see the contents were actually mixing, and both materials shrug off constant immersion.

Results

~1.5 rpmMixing error at setpoint, 3000 cP
IPX-5Passed water-ingress test
4 signalsTemp, pressure, methane, pH

Validation and limits

  • Temperature validated against a written protocol at five setpoints from 30 to 50 °C: steady-state error stayed between 0.04 and 0.10 °C, cross-checked against a separate thermometer.
  • Mixing held within about 1.5 rpm of setpoint at 100 and 120 rpm with under 1 rpm of spread, confirmed by tachometer; the sealed unit passed an IPX-5 water-ingress test.
  • Temperature precision floored near 0.1 °C, a limit of the sensors rather than the controller; reaching 0.01 °C would need better instrumentation.
  • In 3000 cP fluid the motor was torque-limited and could not reach its 150 rpm top speed, holding about 142, and the methane sensor saturates at 10,000 ppm; both are the first items for a next revision.
02 / Graduate FEA · Nonlinear and contact

Engine Gasket Hyperelastic FEA

An engine head gasket seals by being crushed, but rubber's large-strain, nearly-incompressible behavior makes that hard to predict. Solo, I calibrated a hyperelastic model from real tension, shear, and biaxial test data (Ogden N=2, R² 0.9629, beating Mooney-Rivlin), ran the full nonlinear contact compression in Abaqus, and found the original section left a 2.22 mm gap with pressure spiking at the crowns, an open leak path. I reshaped the cross-section so it seals fully, gap to zero, with pressure spread evenly across the face.

2.22 → 0 mmSeal gap, closed by the redesign, with uniform contact pressure.
HyperelasticityFrictional contactMesh convergenceShape redesignAbaqusMATLAB
Show full breakdownHide breakdown

The problem

The head moves down 12.6 mm onto the rubber gasket with friction on every contact, including the gasket against itself. Rubber is nearly incompressible and strains far past the linear range, so the job is nonlinear in material, geometry, and contact at once, and the as-given shape never seals: it leaves a 2.2246 mm gap with contact pressure concentrated at the crowns.

Approach

From simple-tension, planar-shear, and biaxial test data I fit Mooney-Rivlin and second-order Ogden models by least squares in MATLAB, and chose Ogden N=2 on three error measures (SSE, RMSE, R²). In Abaqus I used CPE4RH plane-strain hybrid elements (a mixed pressure-displacement formulation that avoids volumetric locking on incompressible rubber), confirmed a medium mesh by convergence, then reshaped the section: a 25 mm bulb arc, a 4 mm bulb fillet, and base fillets cut from 3.2 to 2 mm. I ran it as a static step with geometric nonlinearity on and light automatic stabilization, sized to at least 20 load increments so the contact progressed without the solve failing.

Ogden N=2 strain-energy density, four constants identified from the test data.

Key tradeoffs

  • Ogden N=2 over Mooney-Rivlin: closer fit on all three deformation modes (R² 0.9629 vs 0.9105), especially the stiffening at large stretch.
  • CPE4RH hybrid over standard elements: pure-displacement elements lock on near-incompressible rubber and report false stresses.
  • Reshape the section over raising preload: closes the gap at the same 12.6 mm travel without adding bolt load to the joint.
  • Accept about 200 N more reaction force for a full seal: the larger crown pushes back harder at the same travel, a fair trade since closing the gap is the gasket's whole job.

Results

2.22 → 0 mmSeal gap closed
0.9629Ogden N=2 fit R²
0.21%Stress change, med to fine mesh

Validation and limits

  • Model checked against all three independent test modes, not only the one it was fit to.
  • Mesh convergence quantified (0.21% from medium to fine) before trusting peak stresses.
  • Reaction force rose nonlinearly with displacement, confirming a hyperelastic model was necessary; a linear material would have mispredicted the load.
  • 2D plane-strain idealization; a 3D segment would capture end effects at the corners.
  • No thermal or time-dependent (viscoelastic) behavior, which a hot, long-life gasket would see.
03 / Graduate FEA · Plasticity and forming

Deep Drawing Forming Simulation

Deep drawing stamps a flat sheet into a shaped part, and the force clamping the sheet sets a real tradeoff: too little and it wrinkles and springs back, too much and it thins or tears, and the press must be sized for it. Following the NUMIFORM benchmark, I built the full four-step forming simulation in Abaqus (shell elements, frictional contact, springback after the tools release) at two clamping forces. It quantified the tradeoff: the heavy binder cut springback but cost 5× the press force (648 to 3161 N) and 11.22% wall thinning versus 2.11%.

5×Punch-force swing across the binder tradeoff, 648 to 3161 N.
Elastic-plastic FEASpringbackS4R shellsQuarter symmetryNUMIFORMAbaqus
Show full breakdownHide breakdown

The problem

A 350 by 35 by 0.78 mm steel blank is drawn 70 mm into a die. Forming runs fail two ways: contact-driven non-convergence, and results that ignore the elastic springback that sets the real part shape once the tools release. The model had to capture both, at two binder forces, following the standard NUMIFORM benchmark so the results are comparable.

Approach

I modeled the blank with S4R reduced-integration shells (a quarter of it, using two symmetry planes) and the punch, holder, and die as analytical rigid surfaces. Loading ran as four deliberate steps, seat the holder, apply binder force, drive the 70 mm stroke, then deactivate the tools for springback, so each contact state was established before the next loaded it. Plasticity used linear isotropic hardening.

Linear isotropic hardening, with friction coefficient μ = 0.14 on the contacts.

Key tradeoffs

  • S4R shells over solid elements: the blank is 450 times longer than it is thick, so shells capture bending and thinning at a fraction of the cost.
  • Analytical rigid tooling: tool flex is negligible next to the blank's plasticity, and it removes a contact-stiffness unknown.
  • Four steps over one: seating binder contact before punch travel is what stops the rigid-body modes that crash forming runs.

Results

11.22 / 2.11%Max thinning, 8.8 vs 1.25 kN
12.74 / 1.34 mmBlank elongation, 8.8 vs 1.25 kN
3161 / 648 NPeak punch force
70 mmPunch stroke, both cases

Validation and limits

  • Punch reaction cross-checked two ways, history output versus summed field reactions (647.3 vs 647.8 N).
  • Deformed shapes mirrored across the symmetry planes and checked for false constraint at the edges.
  • Isotropic hardening misses the Bauschinger effect on the reverse bend at the die radius; kinematic hardening would sharpen springback.
  • No sheet anisotropy or forming-limit check, both of which matter for a real production blank.
04 / Mechanical design project

No-Slip Walker, Fall Prevention

The No-Slip Walker is a smart rollator that senses a fall and locks to arrest it. I owned the structural side: the frame and locking mechanism, the stress analysis, the FMEA-driven material choice (6061-T6), and the GD&T drawings. The analysis did its real job by exposing a problem, under the bounding 294.7 lbf handle load the upper frame reaches yield at a factor of safety of 1.00, no margin, so I flagged it as the redesign priority and the basis for a safe weight limit.

294.7 lbfLoad at which the upper frame yields, the binding limit my analysis set (hand calc matched the FEA).
Structural FEAANSYSHand-calc cross-checkFMEA6061-T6GD&T
Show full breakdownHide breakdown

The problem

A fall happens faster than a user can react, so the walker has to detect and arrest it, which means the frame can suddenly be loaded like a structure, not a walking aid. My job was to prove the frame survives a conservative worst case, choose a material that is strong, light, and corrosion-resistant, and release manufacturable GD&T drawings, while finding any member that does not have enough margin.

Approach

I ran ANSYS static structural analysis on all five load-bearing members under two bounding cases, standing (full load through the handles) and seated, then cross-checked the critical result with a hand calculation. The bounding handle case puts the user's entire weight through the handles, which is conservative on purpose, so a member that passes here is safe in normal use.

Factor of safety per member from the ANSYS peak von Mises stress against the 6061-T6 yield.

Key tradeoffs

  • 6061-T6 over steel or polymer: the FMEA ranked stiffness-per-weight and corrosion highest; aluminum carries the load without daily lifting weight, and machines cleanly.
  • Conservative handle load over gait loads: the fall event, not normal walking, is the design driver, so I sized to it.
  • FEA plus a hand calc: the closed-form check confirmed the critical number rather than trusting one contour plot.

Results

FOS 1.00Upper frame, no margin (flagged)
294.7 lbfHand-calc yield load = FEA

Validation and limits

  • The four other members carry healthy margin (FOS roughly 1.7 to 19), and the hand calc agreed with the FEA on the critical one.
  • Even the upper frame is comfortable seated (FOS over 200); only the bounding standing-handle case drives it to the limit, which is exactly why that case is the design driver.
  • The upper frame is not acceptable as drawn: a factor of safety of 1.00 leaves no margin, so it needs a gusset or larger section, or a published weight limit, before use.
  • Static analysis only; a real fall is dynamic and would apply an impact factor above the static load.
05 / Fluid mechanics · hydraulic system design

Centrifugal Pump and Piping System

A hydraulic press needed oil delivered at 30 to 40 GPM and 50 psia, and the task was to design the whole loop around it: size the pump, size the return line, decide whether the oil could drain back to the tank under gravity alone, specify a throttling valve, and keep the cost reasonable. I built the head budget term by term to a total dynamic head of 36.0 m (118 ft) and chose a 3 hp pump whose curve clears it, showed the return runs on gravity with no second pump, and sized a globe valve to the exact flow coefficient the system needs.

36.0 mTotal dynamic head the pump must supply, summed term by term.
Energy balanceDarcy-WeisbachReynolds regimeValve sizing (Cv)Iterative MATLABPump selection
Show full breakdownHide breakdown

The problem

A press that needs a steady 30 to 40 GPM at 50 psia sets off a chain of decisions. The pump can only be chosen once you know the total head it must overcome, and that head turns out to be dominated by the pressurized press, not the plumbing. The return side is a separate question: can gravity carry the oil back to the tank, or is a second pump required? And the valve has to hold the flow in band without throwing away pressure. Get the flow regime wrong and every friction term, and the whole selection, goes with it.

Approach

I wrote a mechanical energy balance between the supply tank and the press and summed every head: the 50 psia pressure rise, the elevation change, pipe friction from Darcy-Weisbach, fitting losses from tabulated K-factors, and the kinetic term. I checked the Reynolds number in both lines first, found them below 4000, and used the laminar relation f = 64/Re instead of a turbulent correlation. For the return line I wrote a MATLAB loop that sweeps pipe diameter, solving for velocity, Reynolds number, friction factor, and head loss, then a new velocity from Torricelli's equation under a relaxation factor, iterating until the velocity settled within 1 percent. The valve I sized from the real discharge velocity and loss coefficient rather than an assumed pressure drop.

Total dynamic head, with f = 64/Re used after the laminar regime check. The 50 psia pressure head alone is 28.5 m of the 36.0 m total.
Valve flow coefficient from the 6.3 ft/s discharge velocity and a globe-valve K of 7.1, a 1.65 psi drop at 40 GPM.

Key tradeoffs

  • Gravity return over a pumped return: any line 2 in or larger drains under the 8 ft of available head, so a second pump, its controls, and its maintenance all come off the bill.
  • 2 in return over a larger pipe: 2 in is the smallest size that still drains by gravity, which holds down pipe cost; the tradeoff is that the margin thins as the tank empties.
  • Laminar f = 64/Re over a turbulent correlation: the regime check came first, so the friction head is trustworthy.
  • Catalog pump and Cv-matched valve over custom parts: both meet the duty point with margin and are easy to source.

Results

Cv 29Globe valve, exact catalog match

Validation and limits

  • Pump checked against the manufacturer's published curve: 36.6 m at 40 GPM clears the 36.0 m requirement.
  • Return-line sweep confirmed every diameter 2 in and up loses less than the 8 ft of available head, so it drains by gravity.
  • Valve coefficient matched to a real catalog part, a Honeywell globe valve at Cv 29, integrated into the 2 in line with reducing bushings.
  • Steady-state only; startup transients and water hammer on valve closure are not captured.
  • The 2 in return sits close to the available-head limit, so the gravity margin thins as the tank drains, and a few minor losses were neglected, which makes the real loss slightly higher.
  • Suction-side cavitation margin (NPSH) is the next check before purchase.
06 / Manufacturing · Machining and drawings

Air Engine, Built and Documented

A compressed-air piston engine, machined from raw stock and assembled to actually run, plus the slicer attachment it drives. On a manufacturing-lab team I owned the machining and the documentation. I cut the piston, block, and flywheel on manual mills and lathes, holding the running fits to 0.001 in, the difference between an engine that runs and one that binds or leaks, authored the assembly, exploded, and detail drawings with the BOM through revisions, and inspected every critical dimension before assembly.

0.001 inTolerance held on the critical running fits, on manual machines.
Manual mill & latheDrawing packageBOMDimensional inspectionSolidWorks
Show full breakdownHide breakdown

The problem

An air engine only runs if the parts truly fit: the piston has to slide in its bore without binding or leaking, the flywheel has to seat true, and every interface has to land in tolerance on a manual machine, with no CNC compensation and no spare stock. The drawings also had to be good enough for someone else to rebuild it.

Approach

I assigned tolerances by function, not by habit: the piston-bore running fit and the shaft seats carry 0.001 in because leakage and runout live there, while non-mating features carry open tolerances so machining time goes where it matters. I wrote each part's process plan, facing, turning, drilling, boring, milling, before touching stock, and fed inspection results back into drawing revisions.

Key tradeoffs

  • Tight only at the fits, open elsewhere: tolerancing everything tightly multiplies machine time and scrap with no benefit.
  • Drill then single-point bore over drill or ream: boring corrects drill wander and hits location and diameter together.
  • Revision-lettered drawings over redlines: the package stays matched to the parts on the bench, the discipline production demands.

Results

Validation and limits

  • Every critical dimension measured and dispositioned before assembly; the finished engine ran on shop air.
  • No surface-finish (Ra) callouts on the bore yet; specifying them would fully control the leakage path by drawing.
  • Inspection used hand metrology; CMM verification of the position tolerances is the production-grade follow-on.
07 / Controls and numerical methods · MATLAB

Optimal Control of Nonlinear Systems

Two control problems that break naive solvers, derived and coded from scratch. For a hyper-sensitive problem, whose solution grows and decays exponentially, I derived the exact answer by hand, then built an indirect multiple-shooting solver that matched it to better than 4e-10 across six time horizons where simple single-shooting diverges. For a six-state robot arm with bounded torques, I wrote a direct optimizer that drove the minimum maneuver time from 12.14 s down to 9.63 s.

< 4e-10Terminal-state error against the exact solution, across all six horizons.
Pontryagin principleMultiple shootingDirect shootingConstrained optimizationMATLAB
Show full breakdownHide breakdown

The problem

Some optimal-control problems are numerically hostile: the equations that define the best trajectory grow and shrink exponentially, so a single shot from one end loses all accuracy by the other. The robot-arm problem is different, a minimum-time move of a nonlinear, six-state arm whose three motors are torque-limited, with no closed-form answer at all.

Approach

I derived the optimality conditions from the Hamiltonian by hand, including the exact closed-form solution to use as a truth reference. I then split the time horizon into short intervals and solved them together (multiple shooting), keeping every integration well conditioned. For the robot arm I parameterized the torques and minimized the maneuver time directly, enforcing the torque limits at 200 points along each control.

Pontryagin minimum principle: the state and costate boundary-value problem, solved analytically, then numerically.

Key tradeoffs

  • Multiple shooting over single shooting: short, well-conditioned intervals survive the exponential growth that destroys a single shot.
  • Direct method for the robot arm: it handles the torque bounds and a free final time natively, where the indirect method gets brittle.
  • An exact reference over convergence alone: comparing to the closed-form answer turns "it converged" into a measured error.

Results

Validation and limits

  • Numerical states, controls, and costates overlaid on the exact solution; the terminal constraint held to under 4e-10.
  • The direct method gives an upper bound on the true minimum time; a collocation method would certify how close 9.63 s is to optimal.
  • The smooth control parameterization may round off a true bang-bang optimum, which an indirect pass could confirm.
